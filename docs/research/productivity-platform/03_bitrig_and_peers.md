# App Workshop: what to copy from Bitrig and its peers

> Corrections from [`06_factcheck.md`](06_factcheck.md) are applied inline and marked **[FC]**.

**INFERENCE — decision:** Copy Bitrig’s guided path from an idea to a real, inspectable app: clarify the job in plain words, turn it into a visible plan, build a small native slice, run it in a real Xcode Simulator, and keep the path to release understandable. For App Workshop, tie that path to the three surfaces the owner named: **USER** experience, **CUSTOMER** purchase and account lifecycle, and **CREATOR** operations.

Keep App Workshop’s edge in evidence: native quality across the selected devices, honest readiness and review status, and a clear handoff that belongs to the client. Bitrig is stronger today at running the whole build loop and automating Apple’s distribution setup. App Workshop should learn from that breadth while keeping human decisions, client data, and actual acceptance evidence visible.

*Labels below: FACT means a source-backed statement, with its URL and check date. INFERENCE means a recommendation or conclusion drawn from those facts. User reviews are anecdotal evidence, not a representative survey.*

## 1. Bitrig: what it does, and where it is stronger

**FACT — team:** Bitrig is made by a team with Apple SwiftUI experience. Its launch team was Kyle Macomber, Jacob Xiao, and Tim Donnelly; Macomber and Xiao are described as SwiftUI co-creators. The current team page also lists Matt Ricketson as a co-founder. [Bitrig About](https://bitrig.com/about), [Y Combinator profile](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/bitrig) — checked 2026-10-19.

**FACT — product path:** Bitrig’s product has grown from an iPhone prompt-and-preview app into a Mac development environment with an iPhone companion. Its Mac app builds real Xcode projects; Bitrig Remote connects to Bitrig Agent on the Mac and streams an Xcode Simulator preview back to the iPhone. The team says Apple found the original iPhone app’s downloaded-code approach noncompliant with App Review Guideline 2.5.2, prompting the Remote approach. [Bitrig Agent](https://bitrig.com/blog/meet-bitrig-agent), [Bitrig Remote](https://bitrig.com/blog/meet-bitrig-remote) — checked 2026-10-19.

**FACT — how the original on-device preview worked:** Bitrig’s 2025 technical posts describe a Swift interpreter: it parsed Swift using SwiftSyntax, translated supported constructs into its own bytecode, and bridged calls to Apple SDK APIs. The current Mac/Remote workflow instead relies on the full Xcode toolchain and Simulator. The original interpreter is useful as a product-design lesson in fast feedback; it is not the current compiled-app release path. [Interpreter overview](https://bitrig.com/blog/swift-interpreter), [bytecode](https://bitrig.com/blog/interpreter-bytecode), [SDK API bridge](https://bitrig.com/blog/interpreter-expressions), [Remote](https://bitrig.com/blog/meet-bitrig-remote) — checked 2026-10-19.

**FACT — editing and publishing:** Bitrig says it supports real Xcode projects, GitHub import and sync, code edits and diffs, builds and tests, and App Store Connect actions. Its publishing flow can create or connect an app record, archive and upload a build, update listing metadata, capture screenshots, and submit for review. It supports iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple Watch, Apple TV, and Vision Pro. [Bitrig pricing and FAQ](https://bitrig.com/pricing), [App Store publishing](https://bitrig.com/blog/bitrig-publishes-to-the-app-store) — checked 2026-10-19.

**FACT — commerce and service setup:** Bitrig documents setting up StoreKit products and subscription groups, and connecting RevenueCat for remote paywalls, experiments, subscription analytics, customer management, and cross-platform entitlements. Its 2026 release posts also describe CloudKit and Supabase backends, push notifications, widgets, Live Activities, Controls, App Intents, Apple Watch, Mac, iPad, and iPhone Duo Simulator support. These are vendor-described capabilities, not independent evidence that every generated configuration is production-ready. [RevenueCat integration](https://bitrig.com/blog/adds-subscription-iap-and-revenuecat), [Bitrig releases](https://bitrig.com/blog), [push setup](https://bitrig.com/blog/add-push-notifications) — checked 2026-10-19.

**FACT — price and requirements:** Bitrig’s current pricing page lists Free at $0 and Pro at $15/month. Free includes 10 daily messages, up to 60 monthly credits, and code export. Pro adds unlimited daily messages and TestFlight/App Store distribution. Both plans use a connected AI subscription or credits; paid Apple Developer membership is separately required for distribution. Bitrig says the Mac app requires Apple silicon and macOS Sequoia or later. [Bitrig pricing](https://bitrig.com/pricing) — checked 2026-10-19.

**FACT — code ownership and limits:** Bitrig’s Terms say users own code, projects, and messages they create, while licensing Bitrig to store, process, back up, and analyze content in anonymized form. Shared projects use a public link and grant link holders broad rights to view and reuse them. The Terms say generated code is “as-is” and must be reviewed and tested. Bitrig can submit to Apple; Apple performs App Review and may reject a submission. [Bitrig Terms](https://bitrig.com/terms) — checked 2026-10-19; [Apple App Review](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/) — checked 2026-10-19.

**FACT — what users praise and complain about:** A few reviews praise the quick, polished native-looking result and describe building useful apps without a developer background. Other reviews and a hands-on 2025 test report nonworking buttons, broken generated screens, repeated bugs, slow responses on larger projects, and failed AI requests. These are individual reports, several from early 2025 versions; they don’t establish how often issues occur in the 2026 Mac/Agent workflow. [App Store review aggregation](https://mwm.ai/apps/bitrig/6747835910), [Tom’s Guide hands-on test](https://www.tomsguide.com/ai/i-tried-an-app-that-makes-apps-using-ai-on-my-iphone-and-it-felt-like-the-future) — checked 2026-10-19.

**FACT — recent direction:** In 2026 Bitrig added Mac and iPad workflows, Apple Watch and extension targets, GitHub support, App Store Connect publishing, RevenueCat automation, push/back-end setup, UI interaction testing, parallel agents, and iPhone Duo simulation. The product is a fast-moving competitor, so its feature list and pricing should be rechecked before implementation decisions. [Bitrig release history](https://bitrig.com/blog) — checked 2026-10-19.

**INFERENCE — where Bitrig is stronger:** Bitrig has moved farther on native build execution, simulator iteration, automated App Store Connect work, and broad Apple-surface support. App Workshop should take those as roadmap pressure, not as proof of quality: make build, device, customer, and review evidence visible for every generated app. A “submit” action should never be presented as “approved.” [Bitrig publishing](https://bitrig.com/blog/bitrig-publishes-to-the-app-store), [Apple submission status flow](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-submissions-to-app-review/submit-an-app/) — checked 2026-10-19.

**INFERENCE:** Combine the best parts without inheriting the “all modules on by default” pattern. The factory should generate only the selected, specified modules and clearly identify their providers, data, and remaining setup.

## 4. Ten ideas to borrow for the owner-operated, later self-serve factory

1. **INFERENCE — guided intake:** Ask who has the problem, what they do now, what result they need first, and why they would return. Offer follow-up questions instead of relying on one all-purpose prompt.
2. **INFERENCE — visible product brief:** Turn answers into a short, editable spec with open decisions and assumptions marked. Let the operator and client agree on the scope before code generation.
3. **INFERENCE — three-side map:** Show the required flows for USER, CUSTOMER, and CREATOR. Keep the creator console in App Workshop’s macOS dogfood app; generated apps should receive the product’s actual customer and user surfaces.
4. **INFERENCE — smallest native vertical slice:** Generate one real task that builds and launches, then expand to iPad, iPhone Duo, Mac, and Watch with platform-appropriate navigation and interaction.
5. **INFERENCE — live compiled preview:** Borrow Bitrig’s Simulator feedback, but make clear whether a preview is SwiftUI canvas, Simulator, or a physical device, and show which compiled build is being inspected.
6. **INFERENCE — point-at-it editing:** Let the operator or client select a screen element in the preview and describe the change; show the proposed diff and affected files.
7. **INFERENCE — simulated acceptance loop:** Exercise important journeys by tapping, typing, scrolling, and checking outcomes. Save the build, device profile, run, and findings as an artifact a person can inspect.
8. **INFERENCE — human-readable release path:** Show the state of metadata, purchase setup, screenshots, TestFlight, and App Review separately. Keep “uploaded,” “submitted,” “in review,” “accepted,” and “released” distinct.
9. **INFERENCE — operator-to-client handoff:** Start with the owner operating the factory for clients. Keep client-owned source, Apple accounts, vendor accounts, secrets, and approval decisions attributable and exportable so a later self-serve workflow has a clean boundary.
10. **INFERENCE — creator console with provenance:** Build the CREATOR surface around revenue, reviews, crashes, releases, analytics, and feedback, with timestamps and source labels. Start with read-only views where possible; show account permissions and data freshness so a dashboard never implies it is complete when it isn’t.

## 5. Five traps to avoid

1. **INFERENCE — promise of instant shipping:** A generated app, working preview, or successful upload is not approval, release, or a first subscriber. Apple reviews submissions and publishes a status; App Workshop should report those states accurately. [FACT: Apple review process](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/), checked 2026-10-19.
2. **INFERENCE — trusting a pretty preview:** A fast preview can hide compile, runtime, persistence, accessibility, or layout defects. Keep acceptance tied to the compiled target and the relevant device surface.
3. **INFERENCE — enabling every module:** The owner has selected RevenueCat as the default purchase layer, but that doesn’t make every app need accounts, cloud sync, push, AI, or analytics vendors. Generate the capabilities that the brief calls for, and show what data each selected provider handles.
4. **INFERENCE — collapsing client and operator authority:** Don’t put client credentials into prompts or make review, price, refund, account deletion, or release decisions invisible. Keep who authorized and who performed an action clear.
5. **INFERENCE — treating customer support as a paywall:** Customers need restore, manage/cancel guidance, refund and support routes, and account deletion where accounts exist. A visually polished purchase screen cannot substitute for these journeys.
