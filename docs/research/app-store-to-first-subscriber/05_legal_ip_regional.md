## Key Question 1 — Age assurance (Texas SB2420, Utah, Louisiana, California AB1043)

### Takeaway
As of 2026-09-27, Texas's App Store Accountability Act (SB2420) is **live and enforceable** (SCOTUS declined to block it on 2026-07-06); Utah's and Louisiana's laws have both been **delayed to 2027** by amendment; California's AB1043 (Digital Age Assurance Act) takes effect 2027-01-01. For a general-audience productivity app with no age-gated content and no minors-targeted marketing, the practical burden today is real but narrow: assign an accurate age rating, be ready to call Apple's Declared Age Range API and honor the signal it returns, don't misrepresent parental-consent disclosures, and don't share age-category data onward. This is an **EXPOSURE/compliance-readiness** item rather than a **HARD-BLOCKER** for a non-minor-targeted utility app in most states today, but Texas's live enforceability makes basic implementation prudent immediately, and the legal landscape is shifting every few months — this must be re-checked at each release.

### Cited Findings
- Texas SB2420 (App Store Accountability Act) was originally to take effect 2026-01-01; a federal district court (W.D. Tex., Judge Pitman) enjoined it on 2025-12-23 as a likely First Amendment violation; the Fifth Circuit stayed that injunction and the law took effect 2026-06-04; the U.S. Supreme Court denied emergency applications to block it on 2026-07-06 (*Students Engaged in Advancing Texas v. Paxton*; *CCIA v. Paxton*) — **FACT, HARD-BLOCKER-adjacent for TX users specifically** (app stores/developers now face live TX enforcement) — [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/07/supreme-court-allows-texas-to-enforce-law-requiring-age-verification-and-parental-consent-on-app/), [CCIA](https://ccianet.org/news/2025/12/judge-blocks-texass-app-store-accountability-act-as-unconstitutional-speech-restriction/), [InfoLawGroup](https://www.infolawgroup.com/insights/2026/7/7/supreme-court-clears-the-way-texass-app-store-accountability-act-is-now-enforceable)
- Utah's App Store Accountability Act: original developer/app-store compliance deadline of 2026-05-06 was removed by legislative amendment; CCIA voluntarily dismissed its constitutional challenge on 2026-04-21 after amendments removed the Utah AG's enforcement authority (enforcement is now **private right of action only**) and pushed the effective date for key provisions to **2027-05-06** — FACT — [Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/challenge-to-utahs-app-store-accountability-act-voluntarily-dismissed-following-statutory-amendments/), [Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2026/05/update-on-utah-app-store-law--another-waiting-game)
- Louisiana's App Store Accountability Act effective date was delayed by one year — from 2026-07-01 to **2027-07-01** — via HB 977 signed 2026-05-15 — FACT — [Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/louisiana-delays-app-store-accountability-effective-date-to-july-2027/)
- California's AB1043 (Digital Age Assurance Act, signed 2025-10-13) takes effect **2027-01-01**; it is architecturally different from the ASAAs — it obligates the OS/platform provider (Apple/Google) to expose a real-time age-signal API at the OS level, and obligates the *developer* to request that signal; enforcement is CA AG-only, with penalties up to $2,500/child (negligent) or $7,500/child (intentional) — FACT — [Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/california-enacts-digital-age-verification-law/), [Kelley Drye FAQ](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/faq-digital-age-assurance-act-california-youth-safety-on-the-internet)
- McDermott Will & Schulte's client alert (2025-12-02, primary law-firm source cross-referencing the actual bill text) states developers under the ASAAs generally must: (1) assign an age rating to the app and each in-app purchase and give the app store a description of what drove that rating; (2) notify the app store of significant changes to terms/privacy policy; (3) use the age-category/parental-consent info the app store provides to enforce restrictions; (4) request age/consent data only at download/purchase time, at a significant update, or to comply with law; (5) use that data ONLY to enforce age restrictions, ensure legal compliance, and implement safety defaults — never share age-category data with any other person; Texas additionally requires the developer to **delete** the app-store-provided age/consent data after completing verification — FACT — [McDermott Will & Schulte](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/app-store-accountability-acts/)
- Age categories under both the ASAAs and DAAA: child (<13), younger teenager (13–15), older teenager (16–17), adult (18+); when an app/store learns a user is in the "child" band it is deemed to have COPPA "actual knowledge" — FACT — [McDermott Will & Schulte](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/app-store-accountability-acts/)
- Apple's own developer Q&A ("Age assurance frameworks Q&A") states explicitly: **"Am I responsible for age restrictions or is Apple? Yes, developers are responsible for their own age restrictions."** In regions where legally required, Apple shares age categories with the developer via the Declared Age Range API, and "you must check the age of the people using your app." Apple repeatedly says "consult your legal counsel" for compliance-scope questions (i.e., Apple will not tell you whether your specific app is in scope) — FACT — [Apple Developer: Age assurance Q&A](https://developer.apple.com/support/age-assurance)
- Apple: full age-assurance framework set (Declared Age Range API + PermissionKit significant-update flow) requires building against the **iOS/iPadOS 26.2 SDK with Xcode 26.2 (build 17C52) or later**; further regulatory-feature APIs (`AgeRangeService.requiredRegulatoryFeatures`, significant-update acknowledgement sheets) arrived in iOS/iPadOS 26.4. The API is available worldwide (not just in regulated regions), and there is no change to the App Review process tied to age assurance — FACT — [Apple Developer: Age assurance Q&A](https://developer.apple.com/support/age-assurance)
- Apple: an 18+-rated app still must implement the Declared Age Range API in regions where legally required — the store-level age rating does not substitute for the runtime signal check — FACT — [Apple Developer: Age assurance Q&A](https://developer.apple.com/support/age-assurance)
- Apple also published region-specific rollout notes: for Brazil, Australia, and Singapore, starting **2026-02-24** Apple will block downloads of 18+-rated apps unless the user is confirmed as an adult (Apple does this automatically at the store level for those three countries specifically); for Utah and Louisiana, age categories are shared with the developer "when requested" for accounts newly created after the respective effective dates — FACT (as WebFetch-summarized from Apple's own "Latest News" post) — [Apple Developer News (Brazil/AU/SG/Utah/Louisiana)](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=f5zj08ey)
- Apple's Texas-specific news post (as of its last update) stated Apple had *paused* store-level Texas implementation plans following the (since-overturned) district-court injunction, and had not yet resumed store-level 18+ blocking for Texas — this is now stale given the 2026-07-06 SCOTUS decision; treat as **UNKNOWN/needs re-check at ship time** — [Apple Developer News (Texas)](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=8jzbigf4)

### Inferences
- For a general-audience productivity/utility app that is not rated 17+/18+, does not target minors, and has no age-gated feature, the immediate practical scope of the ASAAs/DAAA is narrow: mainly (a) answer the App Store Connect age-rating questionnaire honestly, (b) be prepared to call the Declared Age Range API if/when Apple requires it for a storefront the app sells in, and (c) never persist or forward whatever age signal comes back beyond using it to gate features. **INFERENCE** — this reading is consistent with McDermott's summary and Apple's Q&A but has not been confirmed against a lawyer's read of a specific app's product surface, and the statutes still have private-right-of-action exposure (Utah) or AG-enforcement exposure (Texas, Louisiana, California) that a solo developer cannot fully rule out without counsel.
- Because Texas's law is live now and the others are delayed to 2027, the single highest-priority near-term action is Texas-specific: confirm current Apple storefront behavior for TX users and implement at least a basic Declared Age Range API call gated behind `AgeRangeService.requiredRegulatoryFeatures`/`isEligibleForAgeFeatures` so the app fails safe (doesn't break) if Apple's regulatory feature flag flips on for a jurisdiction. **INFERENCE**.
- Given four different state effective dates (TX live 2026, UT 2027-05-06, LA 2027-07-01, CA 2027-01-01) and a currently-unsettled TX store-level enforcement picture, this entire area needs a **re-check immediately before each App Store submission**, not a one-time decision — it is legislatively and judicially the fastest-moving item in this whole catalogue.

## Key Question 2 — Intellectual property (name clearance, third-party brands/mascots, keywords)

### Takeaway
Apple's own guidelines are the fastest, cheapest filter here and they are strict: 5.2.1 bars any "protected third-party material such as trademarks, copyrighted works, or patented ideas" without permission, 2.3.7 bars trademarked terms in app-name/keyword metadata, and 4.1(c) separately bars using "another developer's icon, brand, or product name" without approval. A scenario app that extracts and displays AI companies' proprietary mascots from other installed apps fails on IP grounds, on at least one additional guideline (self-contained-bundle sandbox rule), and is very unlikely to be technically achievable on iOS in the first place — this is a **HARD-BLOCKER** for that specific feature (app either gets rejected or the feature must be removed), not merely an EXPOSURE item, and carries real off-App-Store litigation risk given the mascots belong to well-resourced AI labs.

### Cited Findings
- App Review Guideline 5.2.1 (Intellectual Property, Generally): *"Don't use protected third-party material such as trademarks, copyrighted works, or patented ideas in your app without permission, and don't include misleading, false, or copycat representations, names, or metadata in your app bundle or developer name. Apps should be submitted by the person or legal entity that owns or has licensed the intellectual property and other relevant rights."* — FACT, verbatim from the guidelines — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/), checked 2026-09-27
- Guideline 2.3.7 (Metadata): *"Choose a unique app name, assign keywords that accurately describe your app, and don't try to pack any of your metadata with trademarked terms, popular app names, pricing information, or other irrelevant phrases just to game the system... Apple may modify inappropriate keywords at any time or take other appropriate steps to prevent abuse."* App names are capped at 30 characters — FACT, verbatim — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Guideline 4.1 (Copycats): *"(a) ...Don't simply copy the latest popular app on the App Store, or make some minor changes to another app's name or UI and pass it off as your own... (c) You cannot use another developer's icon, brand, or product name in your app's icon or name, without approval from the developer."* Violations can lead to removal from the Apple Developer Program, not just app rejection — FACT, verbatim — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Guideline 5.2.2 (Third-Party Sites/Services): if an app "uses, accesses, monetizes access to, or displays content from a third-party service," the developer must be "specifically permitted to do so under the service's terms of use" and must provide authorization "upon request" — FACT — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Guideline 2.5.2 (self-contained bundles): apps "may not read or write data outside the designated container area" and may not "download, install, or execute code which introduces or changes features or functionality of the app, including other apps" — FACT — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Guideline 5.1.2(iv) separately bars apps from "collect[ing] information about which other apps are installed on a user's device for the purposes of analytics or advertising/marketing" — FACT — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- If a developer believes another App Store app infringes their IP, Apple's stated remedy is to "submit a claim via our web form" — i.e., Apple's process for IP disputes is complaint-driven takedown, not pre-emptive clearance; App Review does not itself perform a trademark search on submitted apps — FACT — [Apple App Review Guidelines §5.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- USPTO's trademark clearance search is now run through the "Trademark Search system," which replaced the legacy TESS tool (USPTO's own site provides a "Transitioning from TESS" handout, a "Comprehensive clearance search for similar trademarks" guide, and guidance on "likelihood of confusion" as the legal standard for conflicts) — FACT — [USPTO: Search our trademark database](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search), checked 2026-09-27

### Applying this to the "mascot-extraction" scenario
Assessed scenario: an app that displays AI companies' mascots (e.g., a mascot character associated with Anthropic's brand, or a pet-sprite character associated with OpenAI's Codex product) by extracting the art "at runtime from the user's installed apps."
- **IP infringement (HARD-BLOCKER for the feature / high EXPOSURE for the developer)**: Mascot artwork is copyrighted (visual/character design) and the company names/mascot identities function as trademarks or trade dress; displaying them without a license triggers 5.2.1 directly, is analogous to the Apple-specific carve-out in 5.2.4/4.1(c) (using another company's brand/character without approval), and creates real-world exposure under the Copyright Act (statutory damages up to $150,000 per willful infringement) and the Lanham Act (false endorsement/association, since a mascot's presence implies sponsorship by that AI company). **INFERENCE from the cited guideline text + general IP law**, not from a reported case — no specific litigation or App Review rejection matching this exact scenario was found in the sources reviewed (see Gaps).
- **Technical/guideline infeasibility of "extraction at runtime from installed apps" (compounding HARD-BLOCKER)**: iOS sandboxes each app to its own container; a third-party app cannot read another app's bundle or private container contents through public App Store-safe APIs. Attempting this would itself violate 2.5.2 (reading data outside the app's own container / executing another app's code or assets) independent of the IP question, and inventorying "which other apps are installed" for this purpose risks 5.1.2(iv)'s bar on installed-app enumeration. **INFERENCE**, based on documented sandbox/guideline text — this is a strong signal such an app would not pass review even before IP is considered, and there is no legitimate public API this app could be using to achieve the described extraction, which itself is worth flagging back to whoever specified the scenario as likely technically non-viable as described.
- **Reputational/legal risk is asymmetric**: Anthropic and OpenAI are well-resourced companies with active trademark portfolios and legal teams; unauthorized commercial use of their character IP in a paid subscription app is the kind of clear-cut case that draws a cease-and-desist quickly, and Apple's own complaint-driven takedown process (5.2 web form) means the rights-holder — not Apple — is the most likely first mover. **INFERENCE**.

### Inferences
- For app-name clearance generally (not just the mascot scenario): a solo developer's minimum viable process should be (1) a free USPTO Trademark Search system query for the proposed name + close variants in relevant classes (software/SaaS is typically Class 9 and/or 42), (2) a plain App Store / web search for existing apps or products using the same or confusingly similar name, and (3) avoiding any competitor/well-known brand term in the name, subtitle, or keyword field per 2.3.7. This is **AGENT-CAN-DO** as a first pass (an agent can run the searches and flag conflicts) but final risk-acceptance is **HUMAN-ONLY** since likelihood-of-confusion is a legal judgment call and a clean initial search does not equal freedom-to-operate (unregistered common-law trademark rights and pending applications may not surface in a basic keyword search).
- Any feature that displays a real company's name, logo, mascot, or product likeness inside the app (not just the name-clearance question) needs the same 5.2.1/4.1(c) screening applied to every asset, not only to the app's own name — this is a broader "does this app contain anyone else's stuff" review that should happen once per release, not just once at naming time.

### Gaps
- No specific reported App Review rejection, lawsuit, or Apple enforcement action matching an "AI-mascot-display" app was found — this assessment is built from the applicable guideline text and general IP-law principles (INFERENCE), not from a confirmed precedent. A trademark/copyright attorney should be consulted before shipping any feature that reproduces another company's character IP.
- Could not verify current registration status of any specific "Clawd" or "Codex" character mark in a live USPTO record search within this session's scope (browser-only research budget did not extend to running the actual USPTO search-system query) — flagged as **UNKNOWN**, and doing so is a mechanical **AGENT-CAN-DO** step that should precede any final go/no-go decision.

## Key Question 3 — AI in apps (data-sharing disclosure, age rating, provider usage-policy obligations, Copyright Office guidance)

### Takeaway
Apple now requires explicit, up-front user consent before sharing personal data with third-party AI (Guideline 5.1.2(i), confirmed verbatim below) — this is a direct App Review **HARD-BLOCKER** if skipped for any app that sends user data to an LLM API. Separately, both OpenAI's and Anthropic's usage policies impose their own pass-through obligations on any app built on their APIs (disclosure that the user is talking to AI, extra rules for high-risk domains and for products serving minors) — violating those is a contract/ToS **EXPOSURE** (loss of API access) rather than an App-Review blocker, but it compounds with Apple's own rules. The Copyright Office's multi-part AI report establishes that purely AI-generated output generally cannot be copyrighted absent meaningful human authorship — an **EXPOSURE** item for any solo developer relying heavily on AI-generated code or app-store assets who later wants to enforce copyright against a copycat.

### Cited Findings
- App Store Review Guideline 5.1.2(i) (Data Use and Sharing), verbatim as currently published: *"Unless otherwise permitted by law, you may not use, transmit, or share someone's personal data without first obtaining their permission. You must provide access to information about how and where the data will be used. You must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, **including with third-party AI**, and obtain explicit permission before doing so... Apps that share user data without user consent or otherwise complying with data privacy laws may be removed from sale and may result in your removal from the Apple Developer Program."* — FACT, verbatim — [Apple App Review Guidelines §5.1.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/), checked 2026-09-27
- Apple's age-rating content-descriptor taxonomy (post-iOS 26 rating system: 4+/9+/13+/16+/18+) has no dedicated "AI assistant/chatbot" descriptor as of this check; the closest applicable descriptors are "Messaging and Chat" (direct communication features) and, where the AI gives health-type guidance, "Medical or Treatment Information" / "Health or Wellness Topics," which can push a rating from 4+ toward 9+/16+ depending on frequency — FACT (observed directly in Apple's published age-rating reference table) — [Apple Developer: Age ratings values and definitions](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/age-ratings-values-and-definitions), checked 2026-09-27. **INFERENCE**: a general productivity app whose AI features are utility-only (summarizing, drafting) and not framed as a companion/therapy chatbot likely stays at the low end of this scale, but any "chat with an AI" surface should be walked through the questionnaire deliberately rather than assumed benign.
- OpenAI's Usage Policies (effective 2025-10-29) prohibit, among other things, using OpenAI's services to "infringe on intellectual property rights of others," to evaluate/classify individuals by social behavior or biometric traits, or for high-stakes automated decisions in sensitive domains (health, employment, credit, legal, etc.) without human review; there's a specific "Keep minors safe" section banning exposure of minors to age-inappropriate content and various minor-safety harms, and OpenAI states it reports apparent CSAM/child-endangerment to NCMEC — FACT, verbatim summary — [OpenAI Usage Policies](https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies/), checked 2026-09-27
- Anthropic's Usage Policy (effective 2025-09-15) requires: (1) **all consumer-facing chatbots/interactive AI agents must disclose to users that they are interacting with AI, at minimum at the start of each chat session**; (2) "High-Risk Use Cases" (legal, healthcare, insurance, finance, employment/housing decisions, academic testing/admissions, published journalistic content) require a qualified human in the loop before the output is used, plus disclosure that AI helped produce it; (3) "Products serving minors, including organizations providing minors with the ability to directly interact with products that incorporate our API(s), must comply with the additional guidelines outlined in [Anthropic's] Help Center article" — a separate, incorporated-by-reference obligation set for any app that lets minors talk to Claude; (4) Anthropic defines "minor"/"child" as anyone under 18 "regardless of jurisdiction" — FACT, verbatim/paraphrased from the policy text — [Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup), checked 2026-09-27
- The U.S. Copyright Office's "Copyright and Artificial Intelligence" initiative is publishing its analysis in parts: Part 1 (digital replicas, published 2024-07-31), Part 2 (copyrightability of generative-AI outputs, published 2025-01-29), and a pre-publication Part 3 (generative AI training, released 2025-05-09, final version still pending as of this check) — FACT — [U.S. Copyright Office: Copyright and AI](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/), checked 2026-09-27
- The Office separately maintains "Copyright Registration Guidance for Works Containing AI-Generated Materials" (a policy statement originally published in the Federal Register, 88 Fed. Reg. 16,190, March 16, 2023) and lists the *Thaler v. Perlmutter* and *Allen v. Perlmutter* registration-refusal cases as the leading precedents on AI-authorship — FACT that this guidance exists and is current Office policy — [U.S. Copyright Office: Copyright and AI](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/), checked 2026-09-27. The underlying rule that the Office's guidance is well known to apply — that copyright protection requires human authorship, so a work (code, an icon, marketing copy) that is wholly AI-generated with no meaningful human creative input is not independently copyrightable, while human-authored selection, arrangement, or modification layered on top of AI output can be — is **INFERENCE/general knowledge cross-referenced against this primary source**, since the session's rate-limited research tools could not open the underlying PDF policy statement to quote it directly this session; a lawyer or the source PDF should be checked to confirm exact wording before relying on it.

### Inferences
- A solo developer building AI features into a productivity app on top of OpenAI or Anthropic APIs is layering **three separate compliance surfaces**: (1) Apple's 5.1.2(i) consent-before-sharing-with-third-party-AI requirement, enforced at App Review; (2) the AI provider's own usage policy (disclosure-to-user, minors provisions, high-risk-use-case rules), enforced by API suspension/termination, not App Review; and (3) whatever underlying privacy law applies to sending user content to a third-party processor (see Key Question 4). Missing any one is a distinct failure mode with a distinct enforcer. **INFERENCE**.
- Because Apple's guideline explicitly calls out "third-party AI" by name, a "Send to AI" or "AI summarize" button that fires before an explicit, specific consent step (not just a buried privacy-policy mention) is a concrete, checkable App Review risk that should be tested in App Review notes/screenshots before submission. This is a decision an implementing agent can flag and a human should sign off on, since "explicit permission" is a UX/legal judgment.
- If a developer wants any AI-generated code or app-store creative asset (icon, screenshots, marketing copy) to be defensible against a copycat, they should keep a record of the human editorial/creative contribution layered on top of the AI draft — pure "generate and ship" assets carry a real, if rarely litigated at solo-developer scale, risk of being unenforceable as copyrighted work. **INFERENCE**.

- Could not directly access/quote the U.S. Copyright Office's Part 2 report text or the March 2023 registration-guidance PDF in this session (browser tool encountered a file-download redirect that could not be followed); the human-authorship-requirement summary above should be verified against the primary text before being stated as settled law in any user-facing document. **UNKNOWN/needs re-verification**.

## Key Question 4 — Privacy law (5.1.1(i), GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, COPPA 2025 amendments, Kids Category)

### Takeaway
A privacy policy is a flat **HARD-BLOCKER** — Apple will not accept a submission without one, full stop (Guideline 5.1.1(i)). GDPR applies to a US solo developer the moment the app is offered to EU users, but for a non-data-broker productivity app with minimal collection the realistic enforcement risk is an **EXPOSURE**, not a practical blocker, and Apple's App Store Connect privacy-label questionnaire is the actual gating mechanism reviewers check. CCPA/CPRA has revenue/volume thresholds (~$26.6M revenue or 100,000+ CA consumers/households, 2026 inflation-adjusted) that a brand-new solo-dev app will not meet at launch — **not a near-term blocker**, but worth re-checking as the user base grows. COPPA's amended rule is now the single most operationally important 2025-2026 privacy change: it became effective 2025-06-23 and its **general compliance deadline was 2026-04-22 — already past as of this research (2026-09-27)** — so any app that could plausibly be "directed to children" or that knowingly collects data from a child under 13 is *already* out of compliance if it hasn't implemented the new rule, an **EXPOSURE/potential-HARD-BLOCKER** depending on audience.

### Cited Findings
- App Store Review Guideline 5.1.1(i) (verbatim): *"All apps must include a link to their privacy policy in the App Store Connect metadata field and within the app in an easily accessible manner."* The policy must identify what data is collected, confirm any third party receiving user data provides equal protection, and explain retention/deletion and how a user revokes consent — FACT, verbatim — [Apple App Review Guidelines §5.1.1](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/), checked 2026-09-27
- Guideline 5.1.1(ii): "Apps that collect data for a legitimate interest without consent by relying on the terms of the [GDPR] or similar statute must comply with all terms of that law" — i.e., Apple explicitly folds GDPR compliance into its own review criteria wherever a developer invokes a GDPR legal basis — FACT, verbatim — [Apple App Review Guidelines §5.1.1](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- The FTC's amended COPPA Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 312) was published in the Federal Register on 2025-04-22, became **effective 2025-06-23**, and gave regulated operators until **2026-04-22** to reach full compliance (COPPA safe-harbor programs had earlier compliance dates for some provisions) — FACT — [Federal Register: Children's Online Privacy Protection Rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025-05904), corroborated by [Latham & Watkins client alert](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule) (2025-05-14), checked 2026-09-27
- Key substantive COPPA changes per Latham & Watkins' summary of the Final Rule: (1) expanded "personal information" to include biometric identifiers (fingerprints, voiceprints, retina/iris patterns, genetic data); (2) a **new separate parental consent requirement specifically for disclosures to third parties** — and the FTC explicitly stated that disclosures "for the purposes of receiving monetary compensation, advertising, or **developing or training artificial intelligence technology** are never integral to a website or online service," so the separate-consent requirement always applies to those; (3) a written, prescriptive children's data security program requirement; (4) a data-retention-limit requirement (no indefinite retention; written retention policy required); (5) new parental-consent mechanisms (knowledge-based authentication, facial recognition with human review); (6) additional privacy-notice disclosure requirements (must name specific third parties data is disclosed to, not just categories) — FACT — [Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)
- COPPA's underlying trigger is unchanged: it applies to (1) any operator of a service "directed to children under 13," using a multifactor test (subject matter, visual content, use of child-oriented characters/celebrities, language, advertising, competitor sites, age of intended audience, evidence about actual audience — the Final Rule adds that marketing materials, third-party reviews, and comparable-service age data can now be considered evidence), or (2) any operator with **actual knowledge** it is collecting personal information from a child under 13, regardless of the app's general audience — FACT — [FTC COPPA Rule summary](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule-coppa); age-category cross-reference — [McDermott Will & Schulte](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/app-store-accountability-acts/) (noting that ASAA/DAAA age signals showing a "child" user create COPPA "actual knowledge" as a matter of law)
- CCPA (as amended by CPRA) applies to a for-profit business only if it meets at least one of three thresholds: (1) gross annual revenue over $25 million (inflation-adjusted to **$26,625,000 for the 2026 compliance year**, since the statute requires biennial CPI adjustment in odd years); (2) buys, sells, or shares the personal information of 100,000+ California consumers or households annually; or (3) derives 50%+ of annual revenue from selling or sharing personal information — FACT (thresholds and mechanism confirmed via California DOJ's own CCPA page and corroborated by multiple 2026 compliance-guide sources reporting the same $26,625,000 inflation-adjusted figure) — [California DOJ: CCPA](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa), checked 2026-09-27 (page last updated 2026-08-28 per its own timestamp)

### Inferences
- A brand-new solo-developer subscription app will not meet any CCPA/CPRA threshold at launch (no realistic path to 100,000+ CA users or $26.6M revenue in year one), so CCPA is **not a near-term blocker**, but the threshold is a moving target the developer should re-check as the user base scales — this is a "revisit at `operate`" item, not a one-time decision. **INFERENCE**.
- GDPR's territorial scope (Article 3(2), not independently re-fetched this session but well-established EU law: applies to non-EU controllers that offer goods/services to, or monitor the behavior of, individuals in the EU) means a subscription app sold worldwide through the App Store to EU users is in scope the moment it's live in EU storefronts — regardless of company size. For a tiny developer with minimal data collection (no special-category data, no profiling/ad-tech), the realistic risk is aligned to what Apple's own 5.1.1 guideline already requires (accurate privacy policy, consent for data collection, data-subject rights language) rather than a proactive EU regulatory action, but this is a risk-tolerance judgment, not a guarantee — a developer collecting more than basic account/usage data, or doing any behavioral advertising, should have an actual GDPR compliance review (lawful basis, DPA with any processor, EU representative requirement under Art. 27 if applicable) rather than relying on Apple's guideline as a proxy. **INFERENCE — this paragraph is general EU-law knowledge cross-checked against Apple's own guideline text, not independently re-verified against eur-lex.europa.eu this session (see Gaps).**

A general productivity/utility app aimed at adults, with no child-oriented characters, marketing, or content, is the easy case for "not directed to children" under the multifactor test, but the developer should still avoid actual-knowledge triggers (e.g., don't ask for or imply collection of a birthdate that reveals under-13 status without a plan for what happens next). **INFERENCE**.

## Key Question 5 — Open-source licenses (GPL/LGPL/AGPL, MPL, MIT/BSD/Apache attribution, RevenueCat)

### Takeaway
The GPL-family/App-Store conflict is real but well-trodden: the historical VLC-for-iOS removal (2010-2011) is the concrete precedent that GPLv2/v3 code and the App Store's terms clash, specifically over the GPL's requirement that anyone can redistribute/modify the binary versus the App Store's DRM (FairPlay) and additional-restrictions terms — this is an **EXPOSURE/HARD-BLOCKER hybrid**: nothing stops a developer from *submitting* GPL-licensed code, but Apple can and has pulled an app after a rights-holder objects, and a developer who ships GPL/AGPL dependencies without checking compatibility is relying on Apple not noticing rather than on a resolved legal question. MIT/BSD/Apache-2.0 dependencies (the overwhelming majority of iOS-ecosystem SDKs, including RevenueCat's `purchases-ios`) are the safe default — permissive licenses require only a retained copyright/license notice, satisfied by a standard in-app "Acknowledgements" / NOTICE screen, and are not a blocker of any kind.

### Cited Findings
- In late 2010, VLC for iOS (built on the GPLv2-licensed VLC media player code) was challenged by VLC contributor Rémi Denis-Courmont, who argued the App Store's terms (which add DRM and restrict redistribution) are incompatible with the GPL's requirement that any recipient of GPL'd binaries be free to redistribute and modify them; Apple removed VLC from the App Store in January 2011 as a result of this GPL/App-Store terms conflict — FACT, corroborated across multiple contemporaneous reports — [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2011/01/vlc-for-ios-vanishes-2-months-after-eruption-of-gpl-dispute/), [ZDNet](https://www.zdnet.com/), [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2011/01/07/vlc-for-ios-removed-from-the-app-store/), [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/), checked 2026-09-27 via search corroboration
- VLC for iOS returned to the App Store in mid-2013 after VideoLAN obtained the necessary rights/relicensing arrangement to resolve the conflict for its own app (i.e., the underlying incompatibility was worked around by the rights holder, not resolved as a general legal question) — FACT (per search-result corroboration of contemporaneous reporting), — [Reddit r/programming thread indexing contemporaneous coverage](https://www.reddit.com/r/programming/), checked 2026-09-27. **Gap**: did not independently re-fetch a single authoritative account of exactly how the 2013 return was licensed/structured — flagged below.
- A developer analysis (Michel Fortin, "The GPL and the iOS App Store," 2011-01-08) frames the core legal tension as: the GPL requires that no additional restrictions be placed on recipients' rights to copy/modify/redistribute, while Apple's Developer Program License Agreement and DRM protections impose exactly such additional restrictions — meaning "any GPL and LGPL software is intrinsically incompatible with the iOS App Store" under this reading — this is a **named secondary source's legal analysis**, not a court ruling; no US court has definitively adjudicated GPL-vs-App-Store-terms compatibility as a matter of law in a reported decision found in this research. — [michelf.ca](https://michelf.ca/blog/2011/gpl-ios-app-store/), checked via search corroboration 2026-09-27
- RevenueCat's official iOS SDK, `purchases-ios` (the most commonly used third-party commerce/subscription-management library on the App Store), is licensed under the **MIT License** (confirmed by fetching the repository's LICENSE file directly, copyright RevenueCat, Inc. 2024) — FACT, primary source — [github.com/RevenueCat/purchases-ios/LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/RevenueCat/purchases-ios/main/LICENSE), checked 2026-09-27

### Inferences
- For a solo developer building a general productivity/utility app, the practical, minimum-viable open-source policy is: (1) default to MIT/BSD/Apache-2.0-licensed dependencies wherever a choice exists (this covers the vast majority of Swift Package Manager/CocoaPods ecosystem libraries, including RevenueCat); (2) treat any GPL, LGPL, or AGPL-licensed dependency as requiring a specific compatibility check before inclusion — LGPL is generally considered lower-risk than GPL if dynamically linked and unmodified (the classic rationale used by, e.g., FFmpeg-based apps that ship an unmodified LGPL build), but AGPL's network-use "distribution" trigger is the strictest and the worst fit for a closed proprietary app; (3) never assume "I only used a small GPL library" is safe merely because the library is small — the GPL's copyleft and App Store DRM conflict is about the *terms*, not the amount of code. **INFERENCE**, consistent with the cited VLC history and legal commentary, not itself a novel legal conclusion.
- MIT/BSD/Apache-2.0 compliance for a solo developer is mechanical and low-risk: retain each dependency's copyright notice and license text, typically satisfied by an in-app "Acknowledgements," "Licenses," or "Open Source" settings screen (a common, Apple-accepted pattern) listing each package name, copyright holder, and full license text or a link to it. Apache-2.0 additionally technically requires reproducing any `NOTICE` file content and stating changes made to the code, which most dependency-management tooling (e.g., CocoaPods' `-generate_acknowledgements`, Swift Package Manager plugins) can automate. This is an **AGENT-CAN-DO** task (an agent can generate/maintain the acknowledgements screen from the dependency manifest) with a light human review pass. **INFERENCE**, standard open-source compliance practice, not independently re-sourced this session beyond the RevenueCat license text itself.
- MPL (Mozilla Public License, e.g., used by some Rust/Firefox-adjacent libraries) is file-level copyleft — modifications to MPL-licensed *files* must be released under MPL, but the MPL-licensed files can be combined with proprietary code in a "larger work" without extending copyleft to the rest of the app, making it meaningfully less restrictive than GPL for an iOS app that merely links against, rather than: modifies, an MPL component. **INFERENCE / general knowledge of MPL 2.0's file-level copyleft design**, not independently re-sourced against mozilla.org this session — flagged as a gap.

## Key Question 6 — Consumer protection for subscriptions (FTC Click-to-Cancel, state auto-renewal laws, EU withdrawal rights)

### Takeaway
The federal FTC "Click-to-Cancel" rule (the amended Negative Option Rule) is **currently vacated and not in force** as of 2026-09-27 — the Eighth Circuit struck it down on 2025-07-08 for Administrative Procedure Act process failures (not on the substantive merits), and the FTC opened a fresh rulemaking (ANPRM, 2026-01-30) that has not produced a new final rule yet. This is a genuine, easy-to-get-wrong trap: several current blog posts still describe click-to-cancel as settled federal law when it is not. Regardless, **ROSCA (the underlying federal statute) and state auto-renewal laws are independently live and enforceable** — California's amended Automatic Renewal Law (AB 2863, effective 2025-07-01) is the single most important state-level EXPOSURE item for a developer selling worldwide, since it now explicitly covers free-trial-to-paid conversions and imposes multi-year consent-recordkeeping duties. Apple's own subscription-management UI satisfies some mechanics (in-app cancellation path) but does **not** relieve the developer of its own disclosure obligations under these laws — this is a shared-but-not-substitutable-responsibility situation, similar to the age-assurance pattern in Key Question 1.

### Cited Findings
- The FTC's amended "Negative Option Rule" (the "Click-to-Cancel" rule) was vacated by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit on **2025-07-08**, on procedural (Administrative Procedure Act) grounds — the court did not rule on the substance of the click-to-cancel requirement itself — FACT — [Legal 500: "Eighth Circuit Cancels FTC's 'Click-to-Cancel' Rule"](https://www.legal500.com/), corroborated by [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/) and [Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/), checked 2026-09-27 via search-result corroboration of multiple independent law-firm alerts
- As of 2026-01-30, the FTC opened a new rulemaking process (an Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking) seeking public comment "on the need for amendments to the ... 'Negative Option Rule'" — FACT, primary source, current FTC page framing — [FTC: Negative Option Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/negative-option-rule), checked 2026-09-27 (page confirms the rule is presently at the ANPRM/comment stage, not a final operative rule)
- California's Automatic Renewal Law (Bus. & Prof. Code §17600 et seq.) was substantially amended by **AB 2863**, signed 2024-09-24 and **effective 2025-07-01**; the amendment expands the statutory definition of an automatic renewal plan to explicitly include "free-to-pay conversions" (i.e., a free trial that converts to a paid subscription), and imposes stricter consent, disclosure, and cancellation requirements plus a requirement to **maintain proof of the consumer's affirmative consent for at least three years, or one year after the contract terminates** (whichever is longer per the underlying bill text) — FACT — [Kilpatrick Townsend](https://ktslaw.com/insights/alert/2024/10/californias-latest-automatic-renewal-law-amendments), [Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/), [Barnes & Thornburg](https://btlaw.com/insights/alerts/2025/california-expands-automatic-renewal-law), checked 2026-09-27 via search corroboration of multiple independent law-firm alerts
- The EU's right of withdrawal (a 14-day, no-reason-needed cooling-off period for online/distance contracts under the Consumer Rights Directive) generally does **not** apply to digital content once download/streaming has begun, provided the trader obtained the consumer's prior express consent to begin performance during the withdrawal period and acknowledgment that this waives the withdrawal right — this is the standard digital-content carve-out under the Directive — FACT/well-established EU consumer law, corroborated by search-snippet-level sourcing from [europa.eu "Your Europe"](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/consumers/shopping/returns/index_en.htm) and secondary commentary; **not independently re-fetched at full-page level this session** (see Gaps)
- A newer EU requirement was found in search results but not independently verified at full-page level: reported that **from 2026-06-19**, online traders active in the EU must provide a "withdrawal button" on their websites/apps to let consumers exercise cancellation/withdrawal rights more easily (apparently implementing Directive (EU) 2023/2673's amendments to EU consumer-protection law for financial/distance-contract services) — **flagged as INFERENCE/needs-independent-verification**, sourced only from a search-result snippet of a Crowell & Moring client alert whose full page could not be loaded this session (returned 404) — [search snippet reference](https://www.crowell.com/), checked 2026-09-27. This should be independently verified before being treated as a compliance requirement, since a solo developer's subscription paywall UI reachable from the EU could be directly affected if the "withdrawal button" mandate applies to app-based subscription offers and not only e-commerce websites.
- Apple's own App Store subscription mechanics (managed through Settings > [Account] > Subscriptions on-device, or via Apple ID account management) provide the in-app-purchase cancellation flow and are Apple's mechanism for satisfying its own guideline requirements around subscription management; nothing in the sources reviewed suggests this substitutes for the *developer's* own disclosure obligations (clear pre-purchase disclosure of price, billing frequency, trial-to-paid conversion terms, and cancellation instructions) under ROSCA, state auto-renewal laws, or EU consumer law — **INFERENCE**, based on the general legal structure (Apple is the merchant of record/payment processor; the developer is the seller of the underlying service and offer terms) rather than a single source stating this explicitly.

### Inferences
- Because the federal click-to-cancel rule is currently vacated, a developer cannot rely on "Apple's IAP already handles cancellation" as a complete compliance answer — the more durable, still-enforceable baseline is: (1) ROSCA's requirements (clear and conspicuous disclosure of the automatic-renewal terms before enrollment, particularly for a free-trial-to-paid conversion, and obtaining the consumer's express informed consent) remain live federal law regardless of the Negative Option Rule's status, and (2) California's amended ARL (and analogous laws in other states, e.g., New York's, Vermont's — not independently re-sourced this session) impose state-specific disclosure/consent/recordkeeping duties that apply the moment a California resident (or that state's residents) can subscribe, which is true the moment the app is sold nationwide via the US App Store. **INFERENCE**.

- The California AB 2863 "proof of affirmative consent" recordkeeping duty (3 years / 1 year post-termination) is a concrete, checkable engineering requirement: the app/backend should log the exact disclosure text shown and the user's consent action (e.g., tapping "Start Free Trial" / "Subscribe") with a timestamp, and retain that record for the required period — this is an **AGENT-CAN-DO** implementation task once a human has decided on the exact disclosure copy. **INFERENCE**.

## Key Question 7 — Accessibility law (European Accessibility Act, ADA exposure)

### Takeaway
The European Accessibility Act (EAA) took effect (compliance deadline) on **2025-06-28** and, per its own text, does cover a subscription app's purchase flow — a mobile app selling access via a paywall is providing an "e-commerce service" as EAA Article 3(30) defines it (a distance service concluding a consumer contract via a website or mobile-device-based service). However, essentially every genuinely solo developer will fall inside the EAA's **microenterprise exemption** (fewer than 10 employees AND annual turnover or balance-sheet total ≤ €2 million), which exempts microenterprises from the EAA's *service* accessibility obligations — making the EAA an **EXPOSURE-if-the-business-grows** item rather than a **HARD-BLOCKER** for a genuine one-person operation at launch, but a duty that must be re-checked the moment the developer hires or crosses the revenue threshold. US ADA exposure for an app is a real but comparatively low-probability **EXPOSURE** item for a tiny, low-revenue developer — it is driven mostly by plaintiff's-bar litigation risk that correlates with visible business size/revenue, not a proactive filing requirement.

### Cited Findings
- The European Accessibility Act's compliance deadline for covered products and services was **2025-06-28** ("in effect" as of that date) — FACT, corroborated by multiple independent 2025-2026 compliance-guide sources — [Accessible.org](https://accessible.org/), [AudioEye](https://www.audioeye.com/), checked 2026-09-27 via search corroboration
- EAA Article 3(30) defines "e-commerce services" as services "provided at a distance, through websites and mobile device-based services... with the objective of concluding a consumer contract" — this is broad enough to cover a mobile app's in-app subscription-purchase flow specifically, independent of what the app's core function is — FACT, quoting the Directive's own defined term as reported — [Freshfields: "Inclusivity-by-design"](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/campaigns/technology-quotient/inclusivity-by-design--the-european-accessibility-act-as-a-driver-for-innovation/), checked 2026-09-27 via search-result corroboration
- The EAA's **microenterprise exemption** applies to "microenterprises" — defined (consistent with the EU's standard microenterprise definition) as businesses with **fewer than 10 employees and annual turnover or annual balance-sheet total not exceeding €2 million** — and such microenterprises "providing services" are exempt from the EAA's service-accessibility obligations, though at least one source (QualiBooth) notes the exemption "provides relief... but does not exempt businesses from making [some accommodation / good-faith effort]," suggesting the exemption may not be absolute in every respect — FACT for the core numeric thresholds (corroborated identically across Level Access, A11yFlow, UserWay, web-accessibility-checker.com), **the nuance about what the exemption does and doesn't fully excuse is INFERENCE/needs primary-text confirmation** — [Level Access: EAA 2026 overview](https://www.levelaccess.com/), [Accessible.org](https://accessible.org/), [QualiBooth](https://www.qualibooth.com/), checked 2026-09-27 via search corroboration

### Inferences

- This exemption is not permanent or app-store-portable: the moment the developer's business crosses either the 10-employee or €2M threshold (e.g., after hiring a contractor as a formal employee, or after meaningful revenue growth), the EAA's service-accessibility obligations attach — this should be a tracked business-metric trigger (similar to the CCPA/CPRA threshold in Key Question 4), not a one-time decision made at launch and forgotten. **INFERENCE**.
- Separately and regardless of the EAA microenterprise exemption, Apple's own App Review process and Human Interface Guidelines already push toward baseline accessibility (VoiceOver support, Dynamic Type, sufficient contrast, Reduce Motion) as a matter of App Store quality bar and (per this factory's own `AGENTS.md`/`PRINCIPLES.md`) as a design gate — so a developer following the existing app-workshop process is likely already doing much of what EAA conformance would eventually require, even while not being legally compelled to under the microenterprise exemption. **INFERENCE**, based on the app-workshop's own stated principles rather than a new external source.

### Cited Findings
- Apple's own "App information" reference page states: *"China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) requires that some apps possess a valid Internet Content Provider (ICP) Filing Number. The ICP number and metadata on the App Information page should match with the information you have filed with MIIT."*, and separately (per a related App Store Connect status-message reference) an app **"isn't available in China mainland because you haven't provided a valid... ICP Filing Number"** if one is required and missing — i.e., the consequence of not filing is simply non-availability in that one storefront, not a rejection of the whole app — FACT, verbatim/near-verbatim — [Apple Developer: App information reference](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/app-information), checked 2026-09-27
- The same Apple page states that, beyond ICP: **games** distributed in China mainland must additionally secure an approval number from China's National Press and Publication Administration (NPPA) — functionally the "China game license"/"China ISBN" process referenced in the assignment brief; apps with **book/magazine content** need an NPPA internet publishing permit; apps with **religious content** need an Internet Religious Information Permit from the National Religious Affairs Administration (NRAA); apps with **news content** need an Internet News Information Permit from the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) — FACT, verbatim — [Apple Developer: App information reference](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/app-information)
- The same page states: *"Vietnamese regulators require games distributed in Vietnam to be licensed to remain available on the App Store in Vietnam."* — FACT, verbatim — [Apple Developer: App information reference](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/app-information). This does not apply to a non-game productivity/utility app.
- Republic of Korea: a **Rating Classification Number from the Game Rating and Administration Committee (GRAC)** is required only if the app (a) has been given the KR-19 regional rating by GRAC, (b) is in the Casino subcategory with a 17+ rating, or (c) is in the Games or Entertainment category with "Frequent/Intense" levels of simulated gambling, sexual content/nudity, alcohol/tobacco/drug references, or realistic violence — FACT, verbatim — [Apple Developer: App information reference](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/app-information). A general productivity app in a non-Games/Entertainment category with no such content descriptors is out of scope for this requirement.
- The same page also surfaces two adjacent, non-region-gating disclosure obligations worth flagging here since they were discovered in the same source: (1) under the EU's **Digital Services Act (DSA)**, a developer distributing in the EU must disclose whether they are a "trader," and if so, the DSA requires **certain identification information, including address and contact details, to be displayed on the App Store product page to EU consumers** — a real practical/privacy tension for a solo developer who may not want a home address published; (2) apps in the Health & Fitness or Medical category, or with "frequent" Medical or Treatment content in their age rating, must complete a **Regulated Medical Devices declaration** for the EU/EEA, UK, and US — FACT, verbatim — [Apple Developer: App information reference](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/app-information)

### Inferences
- For this scoped app (general productivity/utility, no gambling/gaming/mature content, worldwide subscription), the minimum-viable regional-distribution decision is: **exclude the China mainland storefront** at App Store Connect configuration time (simplest option, explicitly supported by Apple's own tooling — this is a one-time checkbox-level decision, not an ongoing compliance program), and otherwise distribute worldwide without needing Korea GRAC or Vietnam game licensing, since neither applies outside Games/Entertainment/gambling content. **INFERENCE**, directly supported by the cited Apple documentation's own conditional language ("if applicable," "some apps," "games").
- Sanctioned-territories exposure (comprehensively sanctioned countries under OFAC rules — historically Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Syria, and the Crimea/so-called "DNR"/"LNR" regions of Ukraine) is handled almost entirely by Apple/the App Store itself: Apple does not distribute the App Store, and typically does not process payments, in comprehensively sanctioned jurisdictions, and the Apple Developer Program License Agreement independently prohibits a developer from being a sanctioned party or distributing to sanctioned destinations. **INFERENCE, based on general, well-established knowledge of OFAC sanctions and Apple's standard developer agreement terms, not independently re-sourced or quoted from the current agreement text this session** — a developer relying purely on "the App Store won't list me there anyway" as their sanctions-compliance answer should still read the current Developer Program License Agreement's export/sanctions clauses directly, since agreement language and the sanctions list itself can change.
- The DSA "trader" address-disclosure requirement is worth flagging as a **decide-early, HUMAN-ONLY** item distinct from the region-blocker analysis above: if the developer is legally a "trader" under DSA definitions when selling into the EU, a home address may need to be publicly displayed unless the developer forms an entity (or uses a registered-agent/virtual-address service) before EU launch. This interacts directly with Key Question 9's account-holder-identity findings (individual accounts display the developer's own legal name as the App Store seller name) and should be decided at the same time as the individual-vs-organization Apple Developer Program enrollment choice. **INFERENCE**, built from the cited DSA disclosure finding plus the individual-enrollment finding in Key Question 9 below.

### Gaps
- Did not confirm the exact, current definition of "trader" under the EU DSA (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065) as it would apply to a one-person developer selling a $4.99/month subscription — i.e., whether a genuinely tiny solo operation could ever be a non-trader ("consumer-to-consumer" seller) for DSA purposes, which seems unlikely for a commercial subscription app but was not independently verified against the DSA's own text this session. **UNKNOWN — recommend a direct read of Apple's own DSA trader-disclosure help page and/or eur-lex.europa.eu Regulation 2022/2065 before the EU launch decision.**
- Did not verify current comprehensive OFAC country list or Apple's exact current sanctioned-territory exclusion list against a live source this session (relied on general knowledge). **UNKNOWN — low urgency given Apple's own storefront-availability mechanism does most of the practical enforcement, but should be spot-checked once before global launch.**
- Did not investigate other 2025-2026 country-specific requirements beyond China/Korea/Vietnam/DSA/medical-devices (e.g., India's data-localization rules, Brazil's other digital-platform laws beyond the age-rating item already covered in Key Question 1) within this session's time budget. **UNKNOWN/lower priority for a general non-game, non-fintech, non-health utility app**, but worth a lighter-touch scan before a specific country launch if the app later adds payment, health, or data-localization-sensitive features.

## Key Question 9 — Account-holder risks (age of majority, legal-name matching, termination, IP assignment) & export control

### Takeaway
Enrolling as an **individual** Apple developer requires being of legal age of majority in the developer's region, using one's real legal name (not an alias/brand/company name) as the Apple Account first/last name, and a real street address (no P.O. boxes) — get this wrong and enrollment is simply delayed, not rejected outright, but it's an easy, avoidable first-week trap. **Export control is the most commonly missed item in this whole catalogue for a solo dev**: standard HTTPS (`URLSession`) is exempt from Apple's *documentation upload* requirement, but an app using only exempt encryption may still separately owe the U.S. government a **year-end self-classification report** — a distinct federal filing obligation Apple explicitly flags but does not itself handle. IP assignment from any contractor/co-founder is a **HUMAN-ONLY, decide-before-any-code-is-written EXPOSURE** item: under US copyright law, code written by an independent contractor is NOT automatically owned by the person who paid for it absent a signed agreement.

### Cited Findings
- Apple's own enrollment page states: to enroll as an individual/sole proprietor, a developer needs an Apple Account with two-factor authentication, must **"be the legal age of majority in your region,"** and must use their **legal name** in the Apple Account's first/last name fields — "using an alias, nickname, or company name... will cause a delay in the approval of your enrollment." Apple also confirms **"Your name will be displayed as the seller name of your apps on the App Store"** for an individual enrollment (i.e., the developer's real legal name is public on every App Store listing). Required contact info includes email, phone, and a street address — **"P.O. boxes are not accepted."** Enrolling an *organization* additionally requires legal binding authority to bind the org, a legal entity that isn't a DBA/fictitious name/trade name, and (except for government entities) a **D-U-N-S Number**. The Apple Developer Program costs **$99 USD per membership year** (may vary by region/local currency), with fee waivers available for qualifying nonprofits, accredited educational institutions, and government entities — FACT, verbatim/paraphrased — [Apple Developer: Become a member](https://developer.apple.com/programs/enroll/), checked 2026-09-27
- Apple's encryption-export guidance states directly: *"When you submit your app to TestFlight or the App Store, you upload your app to a server in the United States. If you distribute your app outside the U.S. or Canada, your app is subject to U.S. export laws, regardless of where your legal entity is based."* If the app "uses, accesses, contains, implements, or incorporates encryption, this is considered an export of encryption software" subject to US export compliance requirements — FACT, verbatim — [Apple Developer: Complying with Encryption Export Regulations](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/security/complying-with-encryption-export-regulations), checked 2026-09-27
- Apple's guidance is explicit about the standard-HTTPS case: *"Typically, the use of encryption that's built into the operating system — for example, when your app makes HTTPS connections using URLSession — is exempt from export documentation upload requirements, whereas the use of proprietary encryption is not."* The `ITSAppUsesNonExemptEncryption` Info.plist key should be set to `NO` for an app using only exempt encryption (standard HTTPS/TLS) or no encryption at all — FACT, verbatim — [Apple Developer: Complying with Encryption Export Regulations](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/security/complying-with-encryption-export-regulations)
- Critically, Apple's own documentation flags a **separate, non-Apple-administered federal obligation**: *"If your app uses exempt forms of encryption, you might alternatively be required to submit a year-end self-classification report to the U.S. government. (If you use non-exempt encryption and provide documentation to Apple, the self-classification report isn't necessary.)"* — i.e., using ONLY standard HTTPS can trigger a US government (Bureau of Industry and Security, BIS) annual self-classification reporting duty that exists independent of and is not satisfied by anything filed with Apple — FACT, verbatim — [Apple Developer: Complying with Encryption Export Regulations](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/security/complying-with-encryption-export-regulations), which links onward to "How to file an Annual Self Classification Report" (not independently opened this session — see Gaps)

### Inferences
- The single most likely real-world outcome for a general productivity/utility app (standard HTTPS to a backend, no custom cryptography, no end-to-end-encryption feature) is: `ITSAppUsesNonExemptEncryption = NO`, no documentation upload needed at Apple, and the app most likely falls under License Exception ENC / mass-market treatment under the EAR such that no BIS report is owed either — but this is a **classification judgment Apple explicitly declines to make for the developer** ("If you have specific questions about Apple's export process, contact us" for Apple-specific questions; substantive export-control classification is a BIS/legal question). A developer should not assume "I only use HTTPS so I owe nothing" without at least reading BIS's self-classification-report threshold once, since Apple's own text says the report "might" still be required for exempt-only apps. **INFERENCE**, directly flagged by Apple's own documentation as a real possibility rather than a hypothetical.
- Because an individual developer's **real legal name is displayed publicly as the App Store seller name**, a solo developer who wants business-name branding separate from their personal identity (e.g., for privacy, or to look more "legitimate" as a company) needs to weigh forming an LLC/corporation (which requires the more burdensome organization-enrollment path: D-U-N-S Number, legal-entity documentation, a functioning company website) against simply accepting personal-name exposure on the App Store listing. This decision interacts directly with the EU DSA "trader" address-disclosure finding in Key Question 8 — both push toward the same early decision point: **decide business structure (sole proprietor vs. LLC/corp) before Apple Developer Program enrollment**, since changing it later (moving an app's ownership from an individual account to an organization account) is a nontrivial App Store Connect transfer process, not a quick edit. **INFERENCE**, synthesizing the Key Question 8 and 9 findings.
- On contractor/co-founder IP assignment (not independently re-sourced this session; stated here as well-established general US copyright-law knowledge that a lawyer should confirm): under US copyright law, a work created by an **independent contractor** is NOT automatically "work made for hire" owned by the hiring party unless (a) it falls into one of the Copyright Act's narrow specified categories AND (b) there is a signed written agreement so stating — for ordinary custom software/app code, contractor-written code is presumptively **owned by the contractor** absent an explicit written IP-assignment agreement. A solo developer who pays any contractor (a designer, a freelance developer, an AI-assisted contractor, a co-founder who later leaves) for app-related work without a signed IP-assignment/work-for-hire agreement risks not actually owning parts of their own app. **INFERENCE/general legal knowledge, not independently re-sourced this session** — flagged as a lawyer-review item, and as a concrete, cheap, decide-before-any-contractor-starts-work action (a short IP-assignment clause in any contractor agreement).
- Apple Developer Program termination triggers relevant to a solo developer (not independently re-sourced from the current Developer Program License Agreement text this session; based on the enrollment page's framing plus general knowledge of Apple's public enforcement pattern) plausibly include: providing false/inaccurate enrollment information (e.g., a non-legal name), fraud or chargebacks, guideline violations serious enough to be treated as Developer Code of Conduct violations (the App Review Guidelines text captured in Key Question 2 explicitly ties some violations, like impersonation/copycat apps, to "removal from the Apple Developer Program," not just app rejection), and failure to pay the annual membership fee. **INFERENCE — the specific, current, exhaustive termination-trigger list should be read directly from the current Apple Developer Program License Agreement, which is only available to enrolled developers and was not independently accessed this session.**

### Gaps
- Did not independently open Apple's "How to file an Annual Self Classification Report" page or BIS's own EAR mass-market/self-classification-report guidance (bis.gov) this session — the existence and trigger condition for this report is confirmed directly from Apple's own text, but the exact filing threshold, deadline (commonly understood to be around February 1 each year for the prior calendar year), and process were not independently re-verified. **UNKNOWN — high-value follow-up**: fetch bis.gov's self-classification report guidance directly, since this is exactly the kind of "developer doesn't know it exists" federal filing the task is trying to surface.
- Did not independently access or quote the current Apple Developer Program License Agreement (available only to enrolled developers) for termination-trigger language, so the termination-trigger inference above is not primary-sourced this session. **UNKNOWN**.
- Did not independently re-source the work-for-hire/contractor-IP-assignment legal analysis against copyright.gov or a legal treatise this session (time-budget trade-off); this rests on well-established, uncontroversial US copyright-law doctrine but was not freshly quoted from a primary source in this session. **Recommend a lawyer confirm before treating as final guidance to a user.**
